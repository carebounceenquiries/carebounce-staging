          The words on the spec sheet

          Tare is what the empty trailer weighs. GVM (gross vehicle mass) is the most the trailer is allowed to weigh fully loaded — itself plus everything in it. Payload is the difference: what we may actually put inside. Glider: 160 kg tare + 340 kg payload = 500 kg GVM.

          Which number matters when. Payload is the one we care about day to day — it is how much castle, blower and soft play we can load. GVM is the one the law and the tow vehicle care about, because the rules are written against the trailer’s rating, not what is in it on the day. That distinction has teeth: a 650 kg GVM trailer towed behind a car with a 1 291 kg tare is illegal even if it is completely empty, because the rating itself is over half the car’s tare.

          So read a spec sheet in that order: is the GVM legal behind our car, and then is the payload enough for the job.

          The tow vehicle decides this: Mazda CX-3, 2020

          What the car allows. Published kerb weights for the 2.0 petrol CX-3 run about 1 291–1 339 kg — the licence disc gives our exact tare, and that is the number that counts. South African law caps an unbraked trailer at half the tow vehicle’s tare, to a maximum of 750 kg, so our ceiling is roughly 645–670 kg GVM. Mazda rates the car at 1 200 kg braked, and sources differ on unbraked (640 kg to 750 kg), which brackets the same answer.

          What that rules in and out. The 500 kg class is comfortably inside the limit, with about 150 kg of headroom. A 750 kg GVM trailer is out unless it has its own brakes — which is also why the bigger Venter and Karet models are not on this list.

          Recommendation: the Venter Glider. With a CX-3 we only get one trailer and we cannot add capacity later without changing the car, so the extra payload is worth paying for: 340 kg against the Apex’s 290 kg, a spare wheel already included, a dealer on Lynnwood Road, and the better resale if we move to a bakkie. The Apex 500 is the right call only if cash is tight — it saves R5 355, but budget roughly R1 000 of that back for a spare wheel.

          The catch nobody mentions: volume, not weight. The Glider is 840 litres and the Apex 820. A rolled 3 x 3 m commercial castle is roughly a third of a cubic metre, so two castles and their blowers alone come close to filling either one. Realistically this trailer carries one castle, its blower and a soft play set — castle plus soft play plus bubble house in one run will not fit. Measure a rolled castle before we order, and ask Karet what the GVM of the 6 ft Apex 605 and 620 is, since they do not publish it. If a 6 ft body comes in under 650 kg GVM it solves the space problem without breaking the law.

          Also confirm: the towbar already on the car has a rating plate — check it is rated for what we are towing, and that the towball download limit (about 75 kg) is not exceeded once the trailer is loaded nose-heavy.

          What that means for four castles

          A rolled 3 x 3 m commercial castle is roughly 80 kg and a third of a cubic metre. Four of them is about 1.4 cubic metres and 320 kg — which lands us in the worst part of the price curve.

          By sea: 1.4 cbm at R3 200–R4 700 a cbm is only R4 500–R6 600 of freight. By air: 320 kg at the small-shipment rate is R85 000 or more, which is more than the castles cost. Air is off the table for anything but a single sample.

          The catch nobody advertises: fixed costs do not scale down. Clearing, documentation, port charges and the agent's fee are per shipment, not per cubic metre, and they run roughly R4 000–R8 000 whether we import one castle or thirty. On a 1.4 cbm shipment those fixed charges are bigger than the freight itself, and many LCL agents bill a one or two cubic metre minimum anyway.

          So the real question is not which agent is cheapest. Their margins are small and similar, and the mode decides the cost, not the company. The question is whether the order is big enough to absorb R5 000 of fixed cost. At four castles it is not — that is R1 500 a castle before duty. At fifteen or twenty it starts to work, and that is a decision about how much stock we are willing to own, not about freight.

          Pick the agent on clearing, not on rate. Ask all five for the same thing in writing: an all-in landed cost for 1.4 cubic metres, 320 kg, door to Centurion, with the duty rate named and the clearing fee shown separately. The one who answers that clearly is the one to use.

          Two things to check before buying any bubble house

          Blower or sealed? A party dome runs on a continuous blower, like the castles. A camping “bubble tent” is sealed and pumped up once. Many of the cheap listings are the camping kind, and they are not built to be set up and packed down every weekend. Ask which it is before anything else.

          Is 3 x 2 m big enough? That is the standard local size and it seats two to four comfortably, which suits a proposal or a date-night setup. If we are picturing a family in there, measure it against what we are promising on the Experiences page before we buy.

          How the process actually works

          1. Register for a SARS importer code first. A clearing agent cannot submit anything on our behalf without it, and registration takes time. Do this before we order anything.
          2. Pick the route by size. One or two castles to test: express courier or a parcel forwarder, roughly 4–8 days. A few castles: air freight, about 7–12 days. A proper first order: sea LCL, cheapest per unit, about six weeks.
          3. Get the tariff code in writing. Inflatable castles sit under HS 9506 in other markets, but the agent must confirm the South African code and duty rate from SARS Schedule 1 before we order. The duty rate is the single biggest unknown in the landed cost.
          4. Work out the landed cost, not the sticker price. Import VAT is 15 percent of the added tax value, which is (FOB x 1.10) + duty for goods from outside the customs union. Duty is never claimable back; VAT is, once we are VAT registered. Budget 20 to 60 percent above the purchase price for the whole job.
          5. Declare honestly. SARS watches Chinese imports for undervaluation. If the invoice is understated they uplift the value and add penalties on top of the extra duty, which wipes out the saving.
          6. Clear it on time. Entry has to be made within 7 days of arrival, 14 for break-bulk, 28 if it goes into a container depot. Late entry means storage charges.
          7. Start with a trial shipment. One castle, one agent, one full set of paperwork, before we commit to a container share.

          Honest caveat. Every figure in this section comes from freight broker and logistics company guides, not from SARS publications, and they contradict each other on whether duty is calculated on FOB or CIF. Nothing here is a final number — the agent must confirm the duty rate and the VAT basis in writing before we place an order.

          The commercial question underneath it. An imported castle is cheaper per unit but we carry the risk: no local warranty, nobody to repair a seam, and a six to eight week wait if one tears in December. A local maker costs more and solves both. Worth pricing both before we decide.

          Selling liquor in South Africa needs a licence, and doing it without one is a criminal offence. A basket with a bottle in it, sold for a price, is a liquor sale. There is a workaround doing the rounds — call the wine free and charge the same for the snacks — but that is a blog’s opinion, not law, and a priced basket that always contains a bottle is exactly what a regulator would call a sale in disguise. Not worth it on a R200 margin.

          Three clean ways round it:

          1. Bring your own, and we bring everything else. The basket includes the ice bucket, two real glasses and a chiller sleeve; the couple brings the bottle. Costs us nothing, looks identical in the photographs, needs no licence. This is the one to build.

          2. Non-alcoholic. A de-alcoholised sparkling or a good sparkling grape juice. Worth stocking anyway — a baby shower and a fair share of customers will not want alcohol, and it is the same basket.

          3. Partner with a licensed bottle store. They sell the wine, we sell the basket, the customer pays them separately. Only worth the admin once we are doing real volume.

          The perishables

          Cheese, berries and dip in a car boot on a Gauteng Saturday is a short story with a bad ending. Two rules keep this simple:

          Buy sealed, stay sealed. If we assemble a basket of packaged goods we never open, we are putting together a hamper. The moment we start decanting, portioning or making dips ourselves we are handling food, and the premises where that happens needs a Certificate of Acceptability from the municipality. Worth one phone call to Tshwane before we ever make something by hand.

          Two fresh items, bought that morning, in a cooler box. Berries and a cheese. Everything else off the shelf. That caps the waste when a booking cancels, and a cancelled booking is where the margin on food actually goes.

          R500 for R300 of food is thinner than it looks

          R200 gross sounds fine until the rest lands on it: an hour of shopping plus fuel, the waste on a cancellation, and the board, cloth, candle and serviettes that make it look like R500 rather than a packet of chips in a box.

          The fix is not more food. Perceived value comes from the presentation, not from another R80 of cheese. Two changes make the numbers work:

          Make the kit a hire item, like the castle. Board or crate, ice bucket, two glasses, linen cloth, candle holder — bought once, collected with everything else on the same run, costed over dozens of baskets instead of one. That is our existing model applied to this.

          Then food lands around R200, presentation consumables (serviettes, a candle, the card) about R40, and we keep roughly R260 on a R500 basket — with no new delivery cost, because the trip is already happening. If we want the loaded version, sell it at R650 rather than stuffing R300 of food into a R500 price.

          The thing most buyers miss: a 6 ft is usually 750 kg GVM

          Most of the used 6 ft trailers on the market are rated 750 kg. Behind the CX-3 that is not legal unbraked — an unbraked trailer’s GVM may not exceed half the tow vehicle’s tare, and 750 kg would need a car weighing 1 500 kg. So half the bargains out there are not bargains for us.

          There is a way round it, and it is worth knowing. A GVM can be downrated. An owner on a 4x4 forum describes taking a trailer to a testing ground and asking to have it dropped from 750 kg to 500 kg; the inspector checked the chassis disc and the licensing office issued the change. If that works at our testing station it opens the whole used market to us, because we could buy a cheap 6 ft, downrate it, and have the volume we actually need. One phone call to a testing station settles it — ask what they charge and what they need, before we go and look at anything.

          The papers, which is where private sales go wrong

          Original registration certificate, in the seller’s name. The RC1. Photocopies are not accepted. If they have lost it, they must apply for a duplicate at their licensing office before the sale — that is their job, not ours, and it is a reason to walk away if they are vague about it.

          Their certified ID copy and proof of residence, plus a completed notice of change of ownership.

          Check the VIN plate is riveted on and matches the papers. A home-built trailer with no VIN cannot be registered, and at that point we have bought scrap metal with wheels.

          Check it on eNaTIS before paying — registration status, outstanding fines, ownership history.

          Roadworthy: the sources disagree on whether the buyer or the seller provides it and exactly when it is needed. Budget R250–R800 for the test and confirm with the licensing office. Testers are reportedly lenient on a trailer as long as every light works and the VIN plate is riveted on.

          Register the change of ownership within 21 days. Both of us carry that obligation in law. If the trailer comes from another province we will need new plates.

          What to check on the trailer itself

          Chassis rust, especially where the body meets the frame and around the axle mounts. Surface rust is cosmetic; flaking at a weld is not. Bearings: jack a wheel up, spin it and listen, then grab it top and bottom and rock it for play. A bearing service is cheap, a seized hub on the N1 is not. Tyres: a trailer that stood for years has perished sidewalls even with good tread — look for fine cracks and read the date code.

          Every light, with a car plugged in. Indicators, brake, tail, numberplate. Then the reflective tape round the body and the red reflectors at the back, both of which the roadworthy wants.

          Coupling, jockey wheel, lid seals, hinges and gas struts. And ask whether the spare wheel is included — adding one afterwards costs over R1 000.

          What we should do

          Buy used, and buy a Venter. At R12 000–R16 000 against R26 354 for a new Glider or R26 999 for an Apex 610, we free up R10 000 — which is most of a soft play set, and the soft play earns money every weekend while a newer trailer does not.

          Budget the true cost, not the asking price. About R15 000 for the trailer, R800 for a roadworthy, R1 500 for tyres if the sidewalls are cracked, and R600–R1 200 for a bearing service before the first season. Call it R18 000 all in and anything less is a win.

          What we give up: the 12-month warranty, a known spare wheel, and a dealer who fixes it on a Saturday. What we gain is a trailer that has already taken its depreciation — a used Venter holds its value in a way a new one cannot, because the drop happens the day it leaves the factory.

          The order to do this in: phone a testing station about downrating a 750 kg GVM, read our own licence disc for the tare, then look at the 2021 Venter in Springs and whatever I Buy Trailers has on the floor. Those two are the shortlist — one is the best price seen, the other is the lowest risk.

          9. Website hosting — where the site lives, and why it moved

          Decided 5 November 2026. The domain is the address people type; hosting is the computer that actually holds the files and hands them over. They are two different purchases from two different companies, and HostAfrica sells us only the first one.

          We left Netlify over a badge

          The holding page went live on Netlify first, and it worked. Then we found that Netlify’s free plan prints a “Powered by Netlify” badge in the corner of the site and the only way to remove it is a paid plan at roughly R350 a month. That is R4 200 a year to host one page. Not worth it.

          This was not a guess — the page Netlify served was 535 bytes larger than the file we uploaded, because Netlify edits your HTML on the way out to inject its own script and badge. GitHub Pages does not do this. It hands over the file exactly as uploaded, so carebounce.co.za shows our page and nothing else. No badge, no logo, no “powered by”.

          Why GitHub Pages and not Cloudflare Pages

          Cloudflare was the obvious free alternative and we nearly took it. It fails on one technical point that is worth understanding, because it will come up again.

          An A record is one line in the internet’s phone book that turns a name into a number: carebounce.co.za → 185.199.108.153. Computers only understand the number. A CNAME record is a different kind of line that says “go look up this other name instead”.

          Our address is carebounce.co.za with nothing in front of it — the “apex”. A long-standing DNS rule says a name carrying a CNAME cannot carry anything else, and our three Zoho MX records — the records that make info@carebounce.co.za receive mail — sit on that exact name. So a CNAME there would have killed our email.

          Forms — what we gave up, and what replaces it

          GitHub Pages has no server. It hands out files and that is all it does. It cannot receive a form submission, run code, or send us an email. Neither could Cloudflare Pages. Netlify Forms was genuinely the one thing we gave up.

          One honest thing worth saying: for castle hire in Gauteng, WhatsApp converts better than a form. People booking a kid’s party want to chat, not fill in fields. The form is worth having, but do not treat it as a launch blocker.

          Why the real site has a form at all

          The quote form asks the five things we must know before we can price a job: the date, the suburb, which product (castle, soft play or bubble house), the package, and how they heard about us. On WhatsApp we end up asking those one at a time over twenty messages, usually while setting up another booking.

          The date field protects us from double-booking — we see it the instant the enquiry lands. The suburb drives the delivery cost, which is a real variable in our pricing: Centurion and Fourways are not the same job. “How did you hear about us” is our only marketing measurement — without it we will never know whether Instagram is doing anything, and we will be deciding where to spend money blind.

          It also works at 11pm while we are asleep, and it gives schools, churches and corporate bookings a way to enquire without messaging a stranger’s personal number. The short version: WhatsApp wins on conversion, the form wins on information. We want both.

          11. Storage and washing — where the castles and the trailer live

          Prices and hours checked 6 November 2026, from each company’s own website. Self-storage rates move and most of these places only quote on request, so treat the published numbers as the going rate and confirm on the phone.

          The short answer: R600 buys dry storage. It does not buy anywhere to wash. Not at R600, not at R2 000. No self-storage facility in Gauteng will let you run water in a unit, and that is not meanness — there is no tap in the unit, no drain in the floor, and soapy runoff across their yard becomes their municipal problem. Every contract also bans “working in the unit”. So the wash has to happen somewhere else, and the good news in section 11.2 is that it barely needs to happen at all.

          11.1  This is three different needs, not one

          Lumping them together is what makes the problem look impossible. Split out, two of the three are cheap and the third is nearly free.

          a. Somewhere dry for the rolled castles, blowers, pegs, extension leads and soft play. Four rolled commercial castles and the gear is roughly 3–4 m³. A 3 m² unit at 2.4 m high is 7 m³, so even the smallest real unit is enough. It must be dry — damp PVC grows mould in 24 to 48 hours, and mould on a castle is not a stain, it is the end of the castle.

          b. Somewhere the trailer can stand. A 5 ft or 6 ft luggage trailer is about 1.8 m of box and roughly 3 m overall with the drawbar, about 1.3 m wide. That fits a standard 2.5 × 5 m parking bay with room to spare, and it fits inside a 6 m² drive-up unit if the door is wide enough — which is the trick that makes one rental do the job of two.

          c. Somewhere to wash and dry. This is the expensive one, because drying is the real requirement and you cannot dry a castle rolled up. It has to be inflated to dry, which means a clean hard surface of about 5 × 5 m, a plug for the blower, a tap, somewhere for the water to go, and an hour of sun. That is a driveway. It is not a storage unit.

          11.2  The way round it: clean it before you roll it

          Wipe the castle down at the customer’s house, while it is still inflated, before you deflate it. It is already up, already on clean ground, already full of air. Spray the handles, the step, the entrance and the walls with disinfectant, wipe, and let it sit the ten minutes it takes you to coil the leads and pull the pegs. Then roll it dry.

          That costs ten minutes a job and R0 a month, and it buys you two other things. It is the moment you can still see damage — a seam, a scuff, a burn — while the customer is standing next to you and the castle is inflated, which is the only time a damage claim is ever easy. And it means the thing that goes into storage is already dry, which is the entire ball game.

          A proper wash — soap, rinse, inflate, dry in the sun — then only needs to happen every few weeks, or immediately after a wet or muddy job. Do that at home on a sunny Sunday. You do not rent a wash bay for something you do once a month.

          What that leaves you shopping for is dry, secure storage you can reach on a Saturday night. Nothing else.

          11.3  The gate closes before we get back

          This decides it, and it is not price. We are a weekend business. A party that ends at 16:00 or 17:00 on a Saturday puts us back at the yard somewhere between 18:00 and 19:00, tired, with a loaded trailer.

          Censtore’s gate shuts at 17:00. Storage 123’s shuts at 18:00. On a normal Saturday we would miss both. And if we cannot get in, the loaded trailer spends the night at our house — which is the exact thing we were paying rent to avoid, except now we are also paying rent.

          So the first question on every phone call is not “how much”. It is “what happens if I arrive at seven on a Saturday evening?” If the answer is a locked gate, the unit is only worth having for the castles we are not using that weekend, and that is a much smaller and cheaper unit than 6 m².

          11.4  What to ask when we phone

          In this order. The first two rule places out faster than the price does.

          1. After-hours access. Can I get in at 19:00 on a Saturday? Is there a code, a card, or a human who has to be there? What about Sunday?

          2. The door. How wide and how high is the opening, and is it drive-up? Can I reverse a 3 m trailer to it and push it in, drawbar and all? Ask for the measurement, not a yes.

          3. A tap. Is there one anywhere on the property I may use occasionally, and somewhere the water may run? Expect no. Ask anyway — it costs nothing and a small operator may say yes.

          4. A plug. Is there a power point near the unit? Being able to inflate a castle for twenty minutes to check it over or finish drying it is worth real money.

          5. The money. Deposit, admin fee, minimum term, notice period, and what the increase is at twelve months. Rent-A-Store charges no deposit and no admin fee; Censtore wants three months minimum; Storage 123 wants R250 up front. These differences are worth more than R50 on the monthly.

          6. Insurance. Do they require contents cover, and is it their policy or may we use our own? Our OUTsurance cover is public liability — it covers somebody getting hurt, not our castles being stolen out of a locked unit. If the castles are going to live off-site, that is a separate conversation with OUTsurance, and the stock is worth more than the trailer.

          11.5  What we should actually do

          Do not rent anything yet. The castles are not even here. Until the orders land we do not know how many, how big rolled, or whether they fit at home — and every option above is month-to-month or three months, so nothing is lost by waiting.

          When they arrive, measure before you phone. Stand the rolled castles up, measure the pile, measure the trailer with the drawbar. Then we know whether this is a 3 m² conversation or a 6 m² one, and that is a R300 difference.

          Clean at the customer’s house, every single time. Build it into the pack-up routine from the very first job, before anyone has the chance to get used to doing it the other way. It is the single decision in this whole section that saves the most money.

          If we must rent, Censtore at R650 is the one to phone first — it is the only published price under budget, and the only one that says a trailer may live in the unit. Phone Rent-A-Store the same morning for a quote on the 3 m² drive-up, because no deposit and no admin fee on a month-to-month is worth a lot to a business eight days old. Ask both of them about Saturday evening before you ask either of them the price.

          And spend two weekends asking about a yard. A plot in Glen Austin or Kyalami with a tap, a hard surface and a gate is the only thing that solves storage, the trailer and the washing in one payment. Nobody advertises it. It is found by driving around and asking, which is free.
